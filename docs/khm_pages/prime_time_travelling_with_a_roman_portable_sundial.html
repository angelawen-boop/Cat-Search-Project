Vitrine EXTRA #9

Prime Time – Travelling with a Roman Portable Sundial

calendar_month until 14 March 2027

The special presentation Vitrine EXTRA,  which periodically showcases different ancient objects as temporary additions to the permanent exhibition of the Kunsthistorisches Museum's  Collection of Greek and Roman Antiquitiesarrow_outward, focuses in its ninth edition on a Roman Portable Sundial. This is how we learn how portable sundials worked and just how accurately – or inaccurately! – they were able to tell the time.

About the exhibition

Nothingness. Silence. Eternity. Then – a bang. A Big Bang. An inception in space. The beginning of time. Then – movement, matter, stars, planets. Life. In time. Because nothing is without time.

Time structures our lives. Since time immemorial, people have orientated themselves by the natural cycles of the sun and moon. Even in antiquity, people sought ways to make the passage of time visible and measurable. One of the oldest forms of timekeeping is the sundial. Whilst this stationary technical achievement worked everywhere, it was of very little use to the travelling Roman in a surprisingly mobile world – so something portable was needed.

On average, visitors spend around 1.5 hours in the Collection of Greek and Roman Antiquities.

Our exhibition focuses on such an extraordinary piece of technological history: a portable sundial measuring only about four centimetres, dating from the mid-second century CE, which conceals something astonishing inside.
Portable sundial, inside of the lid with an astronomical depiction, ca. second half of the 2nd century CE © KHM-Museumsverband
Particularly noteworthy is the fact that it has been preserved almost in its entirety.

Portable sundials are among the great rarities of archaeology. Only around twenty examples are known to exist worldwide today; of many, only individual parts remain. The object in the Kunsthistorisches Museum is likely to be the most complete known example. 

Particularly noteworthy is the fact that it has been preserved almost in its entirety.

One watch, many parts

A tin …

The top and bottom of our portable sundial are made from a medallion (which was probably sawn apart). The top is adorned with a portrait of the Roman Emperor Antoninus Pius (reigned 138–161 CE), whilst the bottom is thought to depict the myth of Phaethon, who falls from Helios’s sun chariot.

But the real sensation lies inside the case: 

on the base there is a small pin for attaching a total of four metal discs, and in the side wall there is a small opening through which sunlight could enter.

But the real sensation lies inside the case: 

… and four discs

The four interchangeable discs, which feature grid diagrams, name places, and regions of the Roman Empire. The inscriptions ‘ALEXAN’ and ‘AEGYP’, which remain clearly legible to this day, refer, for example, to the city of Alexandria and the province of Egypt (fig. on the right). 

Tip: You can find images of all parts of the portable sundial in our online collection.

How to use it! A user manual

But how exactly did the portable sundial work?

As the angle of incidence of sunlight varies depending on the latitude, the reading had to be adjusted to the user’s current location. The different scales on the bronze discs served this purpose. The current month also had to be known.

Imagine you were carrying a watch like this with you more than 1,800 years ago. First, you would have selected the disc corresponding to your location and slotted it onto the pin at the base of the case. Next, you would have adjusted a ruler – a feature no longer found on our modern portable sundial – to the desired month line. The sundial then had to be precisely aligned. A ray of sunlight entered the interior of the box through the opening in the side wall, creating a point of light on the ruler. Using the adjacent hour line, you could then read the time.
