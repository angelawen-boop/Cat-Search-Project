What a Wonderful World 
Masterpieces from the Musée d'Orsay

From November 14th, 2026 to March 28th, 2027
Book
During the second half of the 19th century, artists – painters, sculptors, decorators and photographers – proved to be particularly sensitive to the changes their world was undergoing. This being so, they sought to depict modern life in their works, and the ongoing evolution of mores, which were redefining artistic codes.

The daily lives of their contemporaries were being transformed by far-reaching changes, with the industrial revolution proceeding at an unprecedented pace compared with the previous century. The railway, which had made its appearance some twenty years earlier, developed at lightning speed, serving French cities for the first time. The rise of large urban communities and the metamorphoses of major capitals changed the landscape once and for all. As France’s capital city, Paris was remodeled at Napoleon III’s instigation and orchestrated in accordance with Baron Haussmann’s plans. 

In this context, a bourgeois middle class emerged with a range of new interests to indulge, including horseraces at the hippodromes located at the city gates, sea bathing and stays in houses in the country.

This new “present time” was an inexhaustible source of inspiration for artists, whether they belonged to the most academic or most innovative movements. The impressionists, who wanted to provide fresh visions of all these fragments of reality, took an active part in this renewal. The daily life of the time became a central theme for a whole generation of artists. With new scenes of interiors bathed in artificial lighting, subjects previously absent from painting made their appearance: the pleasures and joys provided by this modern world. Artists came up with new modes of expression enabling them to better capture the present time and tradition.

Interior scenes, whether they took place at the theater (public space) or in the family drawing room (private space), revealed all the gradations of the fascinating spectrum of intimacies. Hence, awareness of contemporary states of affairs became particularly strong in artistic life. This pictorial representation of joie de vivre expresses a universal reality that transcends eras and cultures.

Parisian nightlife shares center stage in the exhibition: the development of gas lighting, and then electricity, shaped an urban modernity that attracted French and foreign artists alike. By depicting the Paris Opera House’s dancers in all their forms, Degas illustrated intimate scenes while heralding the beginnings of modern art.

Curatorship

  • Édouard Papet, General Curator of Sculpture, Musée d’Orsay, Paris;
  • Natsuko Ohashi, curator, Tokyo Metropolitan Art Museum, Tokyo.
